01 — Vision & Mission

The coordination layer
for Africa's food system.

Beginning in Nigeria with Seasons by TRACE—an owned demand node that proves the customer, supply, operating and data model before network-scale assets are built.

Thesis updated: 2026-06-15
Status: Core
Fresh food loses value not only because storage is missing, but because demand, quality, movement, pricing and inventory are poorly coordinated. TRACE begins with an operating business, not an infrastructure assumption. Seasons creates concentrated demand and a trusted customer experience. The TRACE backend coordinates how products are sourced, received, graded, moved, stored, allocated and sold. Every transaction creates the evidence needed to reduce waste, improve economics and determine how the network should scale.
The mission — three jobs
JOB 1 — TRUSTED DEMAND

Make better local food easier to discover, trust and buy.

Seasons by TRACE begins with a specialty fresh-food market combining everyday produce, seasonal discoveries and selected local food products. The first market is Nigeria, but the model is designed to celebrate local and regional food systems wherever TRACE operates.

JOB 2 — REUSABLE OPERATING SYSTEM

Move food from source to multiple buyers with less waste and better economics.

TRACE coordinates sourcing, supplier performance, quality standards, transportation, receiving, storage, processing, inventory allocation and fulfillment. The same backend serves Seasons, B2B customers and future wholesale or digital channels.

JOB 3 — DECISION INTELLIGENCE

Turn every product movement into better decisions.

TRACE captures price, cost, quality, yield, route, storage history, channel allocation, selling price and waste outcome of every lot. The first purpose is to improve TRACE's own operations. As the network expands, the same dataset supports external market intelligence.

Infrastructure is a response to observed product movement and economic need — not an assumption about where value is being lost. The storefront is part of the infrastructure logic, not a detour from it.
Economic philosophy
01 — PREMIUM STANDARD, DISCIPLINED VALUE

Seasons can provide a premium standard of quality, curation, handling and experience without relying on blanket premium pricing. Everyday staples should remain competitive, while differentiated, seasonal, prepared or convenience products may carry appropriate premiums.

02 — IMPROVE THE CUSTOMER'S TOTAL VALUE

Value includes usable yield, shelf life, pack size, reliability, time saved, transparent pricing and the likelihood the product will actually be consumed. TRACE aims to remove waste and uncertainty that customers currently pay for indirectly.

03 — EARN MARGIN THROUGH OPERATING ADVANTAGE

TRACE earns stronger economics through better buying, supplier discipline, inventory turns, route density, processing yield, intelligent channel allocation and selective private label — not simply by adding a premium to the same produce.

04 — REINVEST TO STRENGTHEN THE NETWORK

Retained margin funds better systems, processing, storage, additional demand nodes and eventually the infrastructure that removes proven bottlenecks. Expansion should be financed by demonstrated economics and operating evidence.

Seasons should feel premium. TRACE should operate efficiently. Neither business should depend on making already expensive food unnecessarily more expensive.
How TRACE sequences the build
Operating posture — Phase 0 through Phase 1

First, prove demand and demonstrate consistent produce volumes using outsourced sourcing, transport and cold-storage partners. TRACE may operate at deliberately modest margins during this phase, passing some efficiencies to customers to build adoption, increase throughput and validate repeat purchasing.

Once demand, route density and operating consistency are proven—and the P&L demonstrates healthy contribution margins and a credible path to profitability—TRACE can raise dedicated infrastructure capital to selectively internalise the most valuable parts of the cold chain, beginning with low-emission refrigerated transport and cold-storage capacity.

02 — The Opportunity

Food is expensive
because coordination is expensive.

Africa does not simply need more cold rooms. It needs a more efficient path between farmers and demand. TRACE reduces cost and waste by improving coordination, logistics, and decision-making — then deploys physical infrastructure where data proves it creates durable value.

Updated: 2026-06-15
Status: Core
Food takes nearly 60% of household income in Nigeria. In 2024, food inflation hit 35.4% — an all-time high. The consequence is not simply that families eat less well. When food becomes more expensive, children leave school, healthcare is deferred, and future earnings are compressed. The food system's inefficiency is not an abstract economic problem. It is a direct constraint on human development. TRACE starts by asking: how much of this cost is coordination failure — and how much of it can be removed?
The coordination failure

Food loses value at every handoff. Farmers lack predictable demand. Buyers lack reliable supply. Consumers absorb the cost of spoilage, inefficiency, and uncertainty. Every participant prices in risk because the system lacks visibility and coordination.

Fragmented supply

Farmers without reliable demand signals

Smallholder farmers produce without knowing who will buy, at what price, or at what quality standard. The absence of forward demand forces short-term, reactive decision-making that compounds waste and reduces farmer income.

Fragmented demand

Buyers sourcing through opaque, inconsistent channels

Institutional buyers — restaurants, hotels, processors — source through informal channels with no quality standards, no price transparency, and no reliability guarantees. Uncertainty is the norm, not the exception.

Inefficient movement

Too many hands between farm and demand

Produce passes through aggregators, wholesalers, and traders — each pricing in their own uncertainty and handling risk. Every additional handoff adds time, cost, and spoilage. The consumer pays for all of it.

Waste priced in

Spoilage and uncertainty passed downstream

Because no participant can predict spoilage, demand, or price, every participant buffers with margin. Waste is not absorbed by the supply chain — it is systematically passed to the end consumer as higher prices.

Human capital at stake

Food affordability is a development crisis

Food inflation erodes real income for low-income households disproportionately. Research links food price shocks directly to reduced education spending, child nutrition gaps, stunted growth, and compressed future earnings. This is not a market friction — it is a development emergency.

A solvable problem

Better coordination, not just more storage

Much of the value lost in Africa's food system is not a function of insufficient infrastructure. It is a function of poor coordination, insufficient information, and inefficient movement. That is a logistics and data problem before it is a capital problem.

Where TRACE competes
Lower effective cost per usable kg
TRACE will not compete on having cheaper transport. It will compete by losing less. Better grading, faster movement, and demand-led purchasing convert higher logistics spend into lower effective cost.
Reliable supply — predictable quantity and quality
B2B customers — hotels, restaurants, schools, caterers — may value reliable weekly delivery more than the lowest headline price. Fewer stockouts, fewer market trips, fewer rejected deliveries is measurable value.
Grade-based revenue from every batch
Traditional markets mix quality levels and sell at one blended price. TRACE routes Grade A to premium channels, Grade B to general B2B, and safe surplus to discount or processing — extracting more total value from the same harvest.
Processing and waste monetisation
Instead of fresh sale or total loss, TRACE creates: premium fresh sale + standard fresh sale + processing recovery. Lower-grade, excess, or near-spoilage produce redirected into cut veg, frozen packs, or processor supply — multiple revenue paths from one batch.

Build the network first. Build infrastructure second.

TRACE starts by coordinating supply and demand through direct sourcing, quality standards, logistics, and operational data. Infrastructure becomes a targeted response to proven bottlenecks — not the first assumption.

The flywheel compounds with scale — every transaction makes the next one cheaper, faster, and less wasteful

01 — Demand Network

Build recurring demand before building assets

Develop a base of recurring B2B customers — restaurants, QSRs, hotels, caterers — who need reliable, graded, consistently supplied produce. Retail complements B2B by improving inventory utilization, generating consumer demand insights, and accelerating learning around pricing, quality, and purchasing behavior.

The thesis: Demand density lowers unit costs and makes infrastructure investable. No hub gets built without recurring demand that justifies the utilisation. LOIs come after demonstrated reliability, not before.

02 — Trusted Supply Network

Verified farmer relationships and supplier scorecards

Build direct relationships with an initial network of strategically selected farms across priority corridors. Grade every lot. Score every supplier. Know which farms deliver quality consistently, which are improving, and which need intervention before a large order is placed.

The thesis: The long-term moat is not farmer access alone — it is knowing which suppliers deliver quality reliably. That knowledge is built through volume and data, not through contracts alone.

03 — Efficient Movement

Route optimization, consolidation, harvest-to-demand timing

Optimize routing, consolidate farm collection and B2B delivery runs, and reduce harvest-to-customer time. Track cost per kg on every route. Remove the days and hands that create spoilage and cost without creating value.

The thesis: Faster, better-coordinated movement can create more value than simply storing food longer. A tomato that reaches a customer in 3 days needs no cold room. The same tomato sitting at a wholesale market for 7 days needs everything.

04 — Intelligence Layer

Data across harvest, grade, route, inventory, price, spoilage

Capture operational data on every lot, every route, every transaction, and every spoilage event. Build supplier scorecards, demand forecasting, route optimization, and quality tracking that compound over time.

The thesis: Every kilogram moved makes TRACE smarter. The intelligence layer compounds with every transaction. Better data leads to better sourcing, forecasting, routing, pricing, and infrastructure decisions — creating a durable advantage that is difficult to replicate.

05 — Targeted Infrastructure

Physical assets only where data proves economic need

Deploy aggregation centres, cold rooms, processing capabilities, and renewable-powered facilities only when operational data confirms a specific, measurable bottleneck that physical infrastructure would remove.

The thesis: Infrastructure should follow utilisation, not precede it. A cold room built on proven demand and route data is a different investment from a cold room built on a market size estimate.
Capabilities — important, but not the starting point

Cold storage, renewable energy, traceability, processing, and export readiness are genuine capabilities that TRACE will build toward. They are valuable after TRACE has proven demand density, supplier reliability, route economics, and quality improvement. They are not where we start.

Cold storage

  • Deployed where loss-stage data confirms storage — not transit — is the primary spoilage bottleneck
  • Sized from actual demand committed by B2B clients, not modelled utilisation
  • Currently Phase 2 planning · 24–36 months from Seasons launch

Clean energy

  • Deployed where it lowers operating cost and improves hub reliability at scale
  • A structural FX hedge — zero marginal energy cost when naira weakens
  • Part of the Phase 2 hub design · not a Phase 0 capital requirement

Traceability

  • Generated through operations from Day 1 — lot tracking, grade records, temperature logs
  • Not sold as the first product — built as the data layer that compounds with network density
  • Becomes a compliance and export tool when domestic operations are proven

Exports

  • Activated after domestic reliability and grading discipline are demonstrated
  • EUDR compliance and international market access follow supply chain proof — not precede it
  • USD-denominated revenue is a long-term hedge, not a Phase 0 revenue line

Processing

  • Added where recurring Grade C volumes and frozen B2B demand create value-capture opportunities
  • The Seasons frozen B2B channel is the first test — blast freeze investment follows proven demand
  • Processing at hub scale is a Phase 2 capability

Pharmaceutical cold chain

  • GDP-compliant pharma zone is part of the hub Phase 2 design
  • Premium pricing justified — NAFDAC compliance and excursion audit trail add real value
  • Requires regulatory engagement that runs in parallel with Phase 0 operations, not before
Why now

The pressure is increasing

  • Food affordability is worsening — 35.4% food inflation in 2024 is not a correction, it is a trend
  • Energy costs make inefficient movement more expensive every year
  • Businesses need more reliable sourcing as formal retail and food service expand in Lagos
  • Climate volatility makes supply unpredictability worse, not better

The conditions are ready

  • Better data tools and lightweight software make network coordination more feasible at small scale
  • Direct farm-to-buyer relationships are increasingly viable without large intermediary infrastructure
  • DFIs and impact investors are actively looking for evidence-backed models — not speculative cold room assets
  • Lagos's growing B2B food service sector creates an anchor demand pool that did not exist a decade ago

TRACE is not starting by building the largest cold room. It is starting by learning where the food system loses value — then building the assets, data systems, and infrastructure that remove those losses.

The model in three parts
The Supply Chain
Direct from farm
No middlemen · same-day or next-day harvest

1 leased refrigerated van at launch. Farm pickups 3× per week from 3–5 farms within 150km of Lagos. Ogun State for leafy vegetables and pineapple. Ekiti for yam. Plateau State for avocado and garlic. The van route is the prototype for the truck network.

The Product
Graded and traced
3 price tiers · farm origin on every label

Every item carries a QR code linking to farm name, state, and harvest date. Three grade tiers — A (premium), B (value), C (restaurant channel) — at different price points. Nothing is sold that you would not eat yourself. Grade C never reaches the shelf.

The Purpose
Prove before scale
12–18 month pilot · data justifies the hub

The outlet runs for 12–18 months as a deliberate learning exercise. Every transaction is data. Spoilage by SKU, grade premium by category, repeat customer rate, farm reliability — all of it feeds directly into the hub investment case and DFI conversation.

Demand does not convert into repeat purchasing

HIGH
Qualify at least two supply options for every priority SKU before launch; establish SKU-level quality standards, origin QC, lot tracking and supplier performance scorecards from Day 1. A single-supplier dependency on any high-frequency SKU is an unacceptable launch condition.
Long-haul logistics are uneconomic at low utilisation
HIGH
Use partner transport and cold storage first — TRACE does not own vehicles in Phase 0. Model route economics at 50/70/85/100% utilisation; define minimum route-activation thresholds; prove corridors through pilot shipments before any asset ownership. A route that only works at full truck is not proven.
Capital committed ahead of operating proof
HIGH
Launch one constrained node; apply formal go/no-go gates for lease, refrigeration, imported equipment and infrastructure; phase capex and preserve operating runway. Execution talent is not in place quickly enough
HIGH
Build a small core team around store operations, procurement and supply, quality and data; use specialist consultants for legal, engineering and systems; do not scale operations until accountable owners are in seat for each function. The operations manager and quality lead are the two hires that cannot be delayed.
Operating data is incomplete or unreliable
HIGH
Start with minimal mandatory fields, controlled SKU/supplier/lot masters and a no-code TRACE Intelligence workflow; test end-to-end traceability before launch; add custom technology only after the operating process works. The data moat only exists if every commercial lot is traceable from purchase to final outcome — no exceptions.
Food-safety or quality failure damages trust
HIGH
Establish receiving and rejection standards, temperature controls, sanitation procedures, batch and lot traceability, incident escalation protocols and appropriate product and cargo liability protections before launch. Power, refrigeration or site infrastructure is unreliable
MEDIUM
Engineer critical loads before procurement; use solar + battery + generator redundancy; establish maintenance SLAs and outage procedures; select a site only after validating power, water, drainage and receiving requirements. Do not sign a lease until power capacity is confirmed adequate for the cool room.

Inventory principle
FEFO — First Expiring, First Out
Not FIFO. The oldest item is not always the next to expire. The item closest to its shelf-life end moves first, always.

06 — Payment rules
Customer payments
Preferred: payment upfront before delivery — especially for new accounts
Acceptable: 50% upfront, 50% immediately on confirmed receipt
Avoid: open-ended credit — "pay me later" is not a payment term in the pilot
Do not extend casual credit even to friendly or promising accounts. The pilot is not a financing exercise.
Operating principle: TRACE should not carry avoidable credit risk during the pilot. The pilot tests demand, quality and coordination — not TRACE's ability to finance customers.
Supplier payments
Small deposit to confirm supply and lock in the harvest — especially for committed volumes
Balance after receiving and quality check at the Lagos hub — not at the farm gate
Deduction or rejection process agreed upfront — both sides know what happens if quality doesn't meet spec before the first order
Fast payment relative to market is a competitive offer to farmers. Use it — but tie it to quality verification.
Fast payment to good suppliers builds loyalty. Slow payment destroys it. Track payment timing as part of the supplier relationship scorecard.

11 — Strategic Guardrails

Seven rules that protect
the thesis as TRACE builds.

These guardrails exist to prevent the most predictable failure modes. They apply regardless of investor pressure, short-term opportunity, or competitive noise. Each is a decision gate, not a permanent restriction.

Applies from: Day 1
Review cadence: Quarterly
Status: Standing policy
A guardrail is not a permanent restriction — it is a decision gate that requires explicit evidence before being crossed. The purpose is to make drift visible, not to prevent growth.
The seven guardrails
01
Seasons is a demand node, not the whole company.
Every investment, hire and process decision should be evaluated against whether it strengthens the backend and multi-channel capability — not only whether it improves the retail experience.
02
The backend must serve at least two channels.
Retail-only infrastructure should be treated cautiously unless it is essential to the customer experience. Receiving, storage, grading, processing and fulfillment must be designed from Day 1 to serve both Seasons and B2B.
03
A category must earn its complexity.
A new category must demonstrably improve at least one of:
Customer frequency
Basket completion
Contribution margin
Throughput
Local provenance
Waste recovery
04
Data capture is part of the operating process, not optional administrative work.
Every commercial lot must be recorded from purchase to final outcome. Incomplete data is not a technology problem — it is an operating failure. Staff accountability for data completeness is non-negotiable from Day 1.
05
No major asset without demonstrated product flow and a measurable business case.
Every significant capital commitment requires evidence of the throughput it will serve, the utilisation it will achieve and the economic improvement it will generate. "We will need this eventually" is not a business case.
06
No second store until Node 1 economics and the backend playbook are repeatable.
A second Seasons node is conditional, not scheduled. The trigger is evidence that Node 1 produces positive contribution economics, controlled shrink, reliable B2B throughput, complete operational data and a documented playbook that does not require the founding team's daily involvement.
07
Private label begins where TRACE has a sourcing or processing advantage.
Private label is attractive as a concept but costly as an operation. TRACE should introduce it only where it directly improves margin through a sourcing advantage, reduces waste through processing, or makes a genuinely distinctive local product more visible.

The core logic

Nigeria loses approximately 25% of fruits and 40% of vegetables post-harvest, primarily from inadequate handling, storage and power infrastructure (World Bank Nigeria Private Sector Diagnostic). Converting even a small fraction of this into finished products creates a large addressable opportunity.

Methodology
Physical logistics — 8–12% of GMV
$10.8bn expanded produce GMV × 8–12% = $864m–$1.30bn. The 8–12% is deliberately conservative given Nigeria's transport environment. Excludes wholesaler trading margins and produce cost.
Cold-chain — applicable unserved flow × value at risk × service rate
Core F&V GMV $5.64bn × 35–50% technically suitable × 90% unserved = $1.78–$2.54bn applicable flow. × 37% World Bank loss rate = $657m–$939m value at risk. × 20–30% service fee capture = $132m–$282m. Less than 10% of Nigeria's fresh food currently passes through a cold chain (FAO AGRIS 2023).
Intelligence — 0.5–1.0% of managed GMV
$10.8bn × 0.5–1.0% = $54m–$108m. Nigeria does not yet have a reliable published market-size figure for fresh-food supply-chain intelligence. This is a planning assumption based on comparable software-layer spend in other agricultural markets.
